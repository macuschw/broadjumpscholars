Physics you can check by hand.

No black box. Every number comes from five plain steps, each built on physics taught in high school and college. The math tab shows every formula.

  1. 1

    Pose tracking

    An AI model finds 33 body landmarks in every frame of the video.

    pi(t) = (xi, yi)

    Pixel positions, every frame

  2. 2

    Smoothing

    Gaps are filled and jitter removed with a low-pass filter run forwards and backwards, so nothing shifts in time.

    fc ≈ 10 Hz

    Zero-lag Butterworth filter

  3. 3

    Parabola fit

    In the air only gravity acts, so the center of mass follows a parabola. Fitting it gives the takeoff velocity.

    x(t) = x0 + vxt
    y(t) = y0 + vyt − ½gt2

    θ = atan2(vy, vx), h = vy2/2g

  4. 4

    Vector geometry

    Body angles come from the line between two landmarks, measured from vertical. The center of mass is a weighted average of 14 body segments.

    α = atan2(Δx, Δy)

    rCoM = Σmiri/Σmi

  5. 5

    Frame counting

    Takeoff and landing are found from the ankle's height (or, as an option, the toe and heel). Times come from counting frames between them.

    T = (fland − ftakeoff) / fps

Every formula, step by step.

The math JumpLab runs on a real jump, in order, each with a worked example from a session we filmed on October 27 (jump 10, 30 fps, phone on a tripod) and the file in the code that does it.

  1. 1

    The scale: pixels to meters

    Mark the two ends A and B of a tape measure of known length L in the picture. The scale is that length divided by the distance between the marks in pixels, by Pythagoras.

    In the code: jumplab/calibration.py, demo/calibrate.js

    s = L ÷ √((xB − xA)² + (yB − yA)²)

    Example. On October 27 the tape gave s = 2.779 mm/px: each pixel along the jump line is under 3 mm.

  2. 2

    The jump distance

    As the rule measures it: from the toe at takeoff to the heel at landing, along the jump. Each end is the median of its marker over several frames (the toe from the bottom of the dip to takeoff, the heel over the first 0.1 s down), so one jittery frame can't move it. d = ±1 is the jump's direction in the picture.

    In the code: jumplab/pipeline.py (jump distance)

    D = s · d · (median xheel − median xtoe)

    Example. Toe at x = 1601.3 px, heel at x = 797.6 px, jumping left (d = −1): D = 803.7 px × s = 2.23 m.

  3. 3

    Finding takeoff and landing

    The ankle's height h over the clip: its ground level b is the 20th percentile (the foot is down most of the time), its rise R the highest point above that. Takeoff is the first frame above b + 0.15R before the peak, landing the first one back below it after. The bottom of the dip is the hips' lowest point in the second before takeoff.

    In the code: jumplab/events.py

    b = P20(h), R = max h − b, hthr = b + 0.15 R

    Why 15%? High enough that walking and tracking jitter never cross it, low enough that it's crossed within a frame of the foot leaving the ground. (On some 30 fps clips the ankle rises with the heel during the push-off, so takeoff reads a few frames early; the toe-and-heel method, an option, avoids that.)

  4. 4

    The flight: a least-squares parabola

    In the air only gravity acts, so the center of mass moves at a constant speed forward and with constant acceleration −g up and down. With one equation per frame and only three unknowns, the frames can't all be met exactly (tracking is a few millimeters off each time), so the fit takes the y0, vy, g that make the squared misses smallest: the least-squares solution. Time starts at the takeoff instant, halfway between the last frame on the ground and the first in the air.

    In the code: jumplab/physics.py fit_projectile

    minimize Σ (yi − y0 − vyti + ½g ti²)²
    A·(y0, vy, g) ≈ y, so β̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀy

    Example. 14 frames in the air, fitted in pixels: vx = 974.7 px/s, vy = 662.8 px/s, g = 3522 px/s². The forward fit x(t) = x0 + vxt is the same idea with a straight line.

  5. 5

    Speed, angle and height

    Takeoff speed is the length of the velocity vector, the launch angle its direction above horizontal, and the rise of the center of mass comes from vy,top² = vy² − 2gh: at the top the vertical speed is zero. The angle is a ratio of two speeds, so it needs no scale at all.

    In the code: jumplab/physics.py, jumplab/pipeline.py

    v = √(vx² + vy²), θ = atan2(vy, vx), h = vy² / 2g

    Example. Times the scale: vx = 2.71 m/s, vy = 1.84 m/s, so v = 3.28 m/s; θ = atan2(662.8, 974.7) = 34.2° (the same in any units); the center of mass rose 17 cm.

  6. 6

    Gravity checks the scale

    The fit measured gravity in pixels per second squared. Gravity is really 9.81 m/s², so that gives a second scale, from physics alone. If it disagrees with the tape's by over 15%, the tape marks, the frame rate or the takeoff and landing frames are wrong, and the results say so. Slow motion saved for slowed-down playback shows up here too: time stretched k times makes gravity k² times too small.

    In the code: jumplab/physics.py gravity_implied_scale, jumplab/checks.py

    sg = 9.81 m/s² ÷ gpx, check |sg/s − 1| ≤ 0.15

    Example. sg = 9.81 ÷ 3522 = 2.785 mm/px against the tape's 2.779: 0.2% apart. Put the other way, the fit found g = 9.79 m/s². Fitting the hip instead of the center of mass gave 7.34 m/s²: arms and legs swing, only the center of mass is a true parabola.

  7. 7

    Timing, and how exact it is

    Times are differences between frames' timestamps. Each event is only known to the nearest frame, so every time carries ±1/fps. Phones recording in a browser space their frames unevenly; when they drift by over half a frame, the frames' own timestamps are used instead of frame number ÷ fps.

    In the code: jumplab/pipeline.py, jumplab/checks.py check_frame_timing

    T = tland − ttakeoff, δT = ±1/fps

    Example. T = 0.468 s at 30 fps, so ±0.033 s. At 240 fps it would be ±0.004 s. That's why slow motion matters for timing and hardly at all for distance.

  8. 8

    The center of mass

    The body as 14 segments (trunk, head, and each side's upper arm, forearm, hand, thigh, shank and foot). Each has a known share of body mass mi and its own center a known fraction ci of the way from one joint pi to the next qi (standard tables from biomechanics). The whole body's center is their weighted average.

    In the code: jumplab/center_of_mass.py

    rCoM = Σ miri ÷ Σ mi, ri = pi + ci(qi − pi)

    Example. The trunk is 49.7% of body mass, centered halfway from hip to shoulder; a thigh 10.0%, 43.3% of the way from hip to knee.

  9. 9

    Perspective: why feet side by side can look apart

    The far foot is about a stance width Δz further from the camera than the near one. By similar triangles, something further away shows up nearer the middle of the picture cx. The camera's distance Z follows from the scale and the lens: a phone's main camera sees about 69° across the long side W of the picture. JumpLab moves the far foot back by that shift before deciding whether the feet were staggered, and allows 30% of it for stance and lens differences.

    In the code: jumplab/feet.py perspective_shift, live/detect.js

    xfar − xnear ≈ −(x − cx)·Δz/Z, Z = f·s, f ≈ 0.73 W

    Example. At takeoff the feet were 650 px right of the middle of a 1920 px picture: f ≈ 1402 px, so Z = 1402 × 2.779 mm = 3.9 m, and the far toe should show up 650 × 0.25 / 3.9 = 42 px = 11.6 cm nearer the middle. We measured 12.5–17 cm on all ten jumps, with the feet side by side; before this correction, every one was called staggered.

  10. 10

    When a jump doesn't count

    A broad jump only counts if the landing is held. Each rule is measured in the jumper's own standing height H, so it works for anyone and even without a tape. The limits came from one example of each foul against 22 good landings, so they're provisional.

    In the code: jumplab/checks.py check_landing, live/detect.js

    Fouls, in standing heights H (for H = 1.70 m)
    FoulRule
    Stepped forwarda foot lands more than 0.22H (37 cm) ahead of the front foot within 1 s
    Stepped backa foot more than 0.20H (34 cm) behind the rear foot within 1.5 s
    Hand downa hand within 0.04H (7 cm) of the floor for 0.25 s, barely moving
    Fella knee within 0.07H or the hips within 0.09H of the floor for 0.15 s

Pixels to meters

A tape measure filmed along the jump line sets the scale:

scale = known distance ÷ pixel distance

Lay the tape along the jump line, on the camera side of the feet. If it has to be nearer to or farther from the camera than the jump, the scale can be corrected by the ratio of those distances (the command-line app does this), because something twice as far away looks half as big:

scalejump = scaleref × djump ÷ dref

Gravity is the built-in check: fitted freely, the parabola should give g ≈ 9.81 m/s².

Why it's done this way

Why fit a curve instead of timing one frame?

Each tracked position is off by a few millimeters. At 120 fps, 5 mm of noise between two frames swings a frame-to-frame speed by about ±0.8 m/s. A parabola through 50 frames averages that out, and physics says the path has to be a parabola, so the fit can't wander.

Why the center of mass, not the hips or hands?

In the air, gravity is the only force on the body as a whole, so only its center of mass follows a true parabola; arms and legs swing around it. JumpLab estimates the center of mass from 14 body segments with standard mass fractions. This demo uses the hip as a stand-in.

Why film in slow motion?

Times are counted in frames. At 30 fps each frame is 33 ms, so a 0.43 s flight could read anywhere from 0.40 to 0.47 s; at 240 fps the step is 4 ms. Slow motion barely changes distances or angles. It is what makes the timing meaningful.
